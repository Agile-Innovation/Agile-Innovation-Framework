# Role, Function and Permission Model

## Definition

The Role, Function and Permission Model defines **how an organization describes roles, functions, responsibilities, permissions, boundaries, and cross-functional interfaces** within the Agile Innovation Framework.

It provides the organizational foundation for trustworthy execution by making required expertise, operational responsibility, authority boundaries, control responsibilities, and participation interfaces explicit.

This model defines the role and permission structure on which decision rights, accountability, consultation, escalation, and independent control can be built.

---

## Purpose

The purpose of the Role, Function and Permission Model is to ensure that all relevant work within the Agile Innovation Framework can be assigned, executed, reviewed, controlled, escalated, and improved in a transparent and accountable way.

It enables organizations to:

* define roles and functions clearly
* make required expertise explicit
* assign responsibilities and ownership
* define permission boundaries
* separate execution, approval, review, control, and arbitration responsibilities
* clarify cross-functional interfaces
* support transparent decision paths
* create the foundation for decision rights and RACI mappings
* reduce ambiguity in collaboration, delivery, governance, and escalation
* support auditability, traceability, and operational continuity

---

## Core Principle

By defining roles, functions, permissions, and interfaces, an organization makes required expertise, responsibility, authority boundaries, and control expectations explicit.

**Roles** define who contributes.

**Functions** define what capability or organizational purpose is performed.

**Permissions** define what a role is allowed to access, execute, approve, review, or control.

**Decision rights** define who has the authority to propose, approve, reject, delegate, review, escalate, or finally decide within a defined scope.

RACI is a proven method for clarifying who is responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed for specific activities or decisions. Within AIF, RACI should be used as a practical mapping method, complemented by explicit decision rights, approval thresholds, escalation paths, evidence requirements, stakeholder participation rules, and independent control mechanisms.

---

## Scope

This model covers:

* role categories
* function categories
* responsibility boundaries
* permission types
* separation of duties
* cross-functional interfaces
* governance interfaces
* documentation expectations
* lifecycle integration
* review and evolution of roles

It provides a reusable governance structure that can be adapted to different organizational contexts.

Out of Scope: This model does not define legal employment structures, contractual liability clauses, or organization-specific job descriptions.

## Role Categories

The following role categories provide a reusable structure for AIF-based organizations.

### Accountable Owner

The Accountable Owner is responsible for the overall outcome of a defined scope.

Typical responsibilities:

* owns the outcome
* ensures alignment with strategic intent
* accepts or rejects major result states according to defined decision rights
* ensures that risks, assumptions, and dependencies are visible
* ensures that required roles are involved

Typical boundaries:

* must not perform independent control of their own accountable scope
* must rely on evidence, review input, and documented decision paths

### Decision Owner

The Decision Owner owns a defined decision area.

Typical responsibilities:

* prepares or leads decisions within a defined scope
* ensures that relevant expertise is included
* documents decision rationale
* ensures that decision evidence is complete enough
* triggers escalation where decision authority is exceeded

Typical boundaries:

* decides only within defined authority limits
* escalates decisions with unresolved risks, conflicts, or threshold breaches

### Product or Service Owner

The Product or Service Owner represents value, usability, service quality, and stakeholder relevance.

Typical responsibilities:

* clarifies product or service intent
* prioritizes needs within defined decision boundaries
* contributes to acceptance criteria
* represents user and stakeholder value
* validates whether outcomes create intended benefit

Typical boundaries:

* must involve technical, security, compliance, and operational expertise before major commitments
* must make prioritization transparent and evidence-based

### Architecture Owner

The Architecture Owner ensures architectural coherence, technical feasibility, interoperability, and long-term sustainability.

Typical responsibilities:

* defines or reviews architecture principles
* validates technical feasibility
* identifies architectural risks and dependencies
* supports technology selection
* documents architecture decisions
* aligns with the Technological Architecture Manifesto

Typical boundaries:

* must collaborate with product, security, compliance, implementation, and operations roles
* must make trade-offs explicit

### Implementation Owner

The Implementation Owner is responsible for delivery execution within a defined scope.

Typical responsibilities:

* coordinates implementation work
* manages delivery dependencies
* ensures that acceptance criteria are implemented
* reports progress, risks, and blockers
* supports iterative delivery and learning loops

Typical boundaries:

* must not approve critical acceptance of their own work without independent review where relevant
* must escalate resource, quality, or dependency risks early

### Compliance and Security Reviewer

The Compliance and Security Reviewer validates compliance, security, policy alignment, risk treatment, and evidence quality.

Typical responsibilities:

* reviews security and compliance assumptions
* assesses control requirements
* validates evidence
* identifies policy gaps
* contributes to risk management
* supports audit readiness

Typical boundaries:

* must be independent enough to challenge implementation assumptions
* must document review results and remaining risks transparently

### Independent Control Role

The Independent Control Role provides review, challenge, validation, or assurance independently from execution responsibility.

Typical responsibilities:

* challenges assumptions
* validates evidence
* reviews accepted risks
* checks adherence to defined controls
* supports auditability and defensibility
* reports unresolved control issues

Typical boundaries:

* must be separated from execution responsibility where relevant financial, operational, technical, legal, or security impact exists
* must escalate unresolved findings through defined paths

### Stakeholder Representative

The Stakeholder Representative represents a defined stakeholder group or affected perspective.

Typical responsibilities:

* contributes domain knowledge
* provides structured feedback
* raises objections or concerns
* validates relevance and impact
* supports acceptance and transparency
* participates in consultation or delegated participation mechanisms where applicable

Typical boundaries:

* must have clearly defined participation rights
* must distinguish consultation, objection, review, and approval rights

### Escalation Owner

The Escalation Owner manages unresolved conflicts, decision blockers, critical dependencies, or threshold breaches.

Typical responsibilities:

* receives escalation requests
* ensures that required evidence is available
* coordinates involved roles
* documents escalation status
* triggers arbitration where required
* ensures timely resolution according to defined escalation paths

Typical boundaries:

* must not bypass defined decision rights
* must make escalation outcomes visible and reviewable

### Arbitration Body

The Arbitration Body resolves conflicts that cannot be solved through normal decision or escalation paths.

Typical responsibilities:

* reviews unresolved conflicts
* evaluates evidence and arguments
* provides binding or advisory resolution according to the governance setup
* documents rationale
* identifies structural improvement needs

Typical boundaries:

* must operate with defined authority, scope, and procedural rules
* must maintain transparency, fairness, and traceability

## Function Categories

Roles may perform one or more functions. Functions describe organizational capabilities rather than job titles.

### Strategic Steering Function

Provides strategic direction, prioritization logic, investment orientation, and alignment with long-term goals.

### Product and Value Function

Defines value, user needs, service expectations, acceptance criteria, and benefit realization.

### Architecture and Technology Function

Defines architectural coherence, technology selection, interoperability, sustainability, and technical feasibility.

### Implementation and Delivery Function

Executes development, configuration, integration, testing, and delivery activities.

### Operations and Service Function

Ensures operational readiness, service continuity, monitoring, incident handling, and service quality.

### Compliance and Security Function

Defines, reviews, and validates compliance, security, controls, policy adherence, and audit evidence.

### Stakeholder Participation Function

Organizes consultation, structured feedback, objection handling, delegated participation, and stakeholder transparency.

### Control and Assurance Function

Provides independent review, challenge, validation, audit preparation, and control monitoring.

### Escalation and Arbitration Function

Handles unresolved conflicts, threshold breaches, decision blockers, and formal dispute resolution.

## Permission Types

Permissions define what a role may do within a defined scope.

### Access Permission

Allows a role to access specific information, artifacts, systems, discussions, or evidence.

Examples:

* read requirements
* view risk records
* access audit evidence
* access roadmap dependencies
* access operational reports

### Contribution Permission

Allows a role to contribute input to an artifact, assessment, decision, or review.

Examples:

* propose requirements
* contribute evaluation criteria
* add stakeholder feedback
* submit risk observations
* suggest roadmap changes

### Execution Permission

Allows a role to execute defined work.

Examples:

* implement a feature
* perform a review
* run a validation
* update a process artifact
* execute an operational procedure

### Review Permission

Allows a role to review artifacts, decisions, assumptions, risks, evidence, or implementation results.

Examples:

* review acceptance criteria
* review architecture decisions
* review security controls
* review SLA performance
* review escalation evidence

### Approval Permission

Allows a role to approve a defined artifact, decision, change, release, exception, or milestone.

Examples:

* approve an Epic
* approve a roadmap milestone
* approve a risk acceptance
* approve a service level
* approve a release gate

### Objection Permission

Allows a role to raise formal concerns that must be documented and addressed.

Examples:

* object to insufficient evidence
* object to unclear acceptance criteria
* object to unresolved stakeholder impact
* object to security or compliance risk
* object to unrealistic resource assumptions

### Escalation Permission

Allows a role to escalate unresolved issues, conflicts, threshold breaches, or risks.

Examples:

* escalate delivery blockers
* escalate security concerns
* escalate dependency conflicts
* escalate priority disputes
* escalate SLA violations

### Control Permission

Allows a role to perform independent checks, challenge assumptions, validate evidence, and report control findings.

Examples:

* verify control implementation
* validate audit evidence
* challenge risk acceptance
* check policy adherence
* review exception handling

### Arbitration Permission

Allows a role or body to resolve defined disputes or conflicts according to the governance setup.

Examples:

* resolve decision conflicts
* resolve stakeholder objections
* resolve priority disputes
* resolve responsibility conflicts
* resolve escalation deadlocks

## Separation of Duties

Effective governance arises from the complementary integration of diverse expertise while keeping execution, approval, control, and arbitration responsibilities clearly separated.

The following responsibilities should be separated where decisions create relevant financial, operational, technical, legal, compliance, or security impact:

* defining requirements
* implementing changes
* approving outcomes
* accepting risks
* reviewing evidence
* controlling compliance
* resolving disputes

A role may contribute expertise across multiple areas, but critical decisions should remain reviewable and should include appropriate independent control.

Cross-functional interfaces are required wherever work depends on multiple functions, perspectives, or control expectations.

### Strategy to Product Interface

Connects strategic intent with concrete product or service needs.

Typical participants:

* Accountable Owner
* Product or Service Owner
* Stakeholder Representative
* Decision Owner

Typical artifacts:

* Epics
* Use Cases
* value assumptions
* strategic alignment rationale
* prioritization records

### Product to Architecture Interface

Connects stakeholder needs, requirements, and acceptance criteria with architecture feasibility.

Typical participants:

* Product or Service Owner
* Architecture Owner
* Implementation Owner
* Compliance and Security Reviewer

Typical artifacts:

* requirements
* acceptance criteria
* architecture assumptions
* feasibility assessments
* architecture decision records

### Architecture to Implementation Interface

Connects architecture decisions with executable delivery work.

Typical participants:

* Architecture Owner
* Implementation Owner
* Compliance and Security Reviewer

Typical artifacts:

* implementation plan
* technical dependencies
* design decisions
* validation criteria
* risk records

### Implementation to Operations Interface

Connects delivery with operational readiness and service continuity.

Typical participants:

* release readiness records

Typical artifacts:

Typical artifacts:

## Minimal Viable Role Model

Organizations may start with a minimal viable role model and evolve it iteratively.

A minimal viable model should include at least:

* Accountable Owner
* Decision Owner
* Product or Service Owner
* Architecture Owner
* Implementation Owner
* Compliance and Security Reviewer
* Independent Control Role
* Stakeholder Representative
* Escalation Owner

This enables a lightweight starting point while keeping accountability, expertise, execution, review, control, stakeholder participation, and escalation visible from the beginning.

---

## Governance Maturity Levels

### Level 1: Role Awareness

Roles are named and basic responsibilities are documented.

### Level 2: Responsibility Clarity

Responsibilities, ownership, and interfaces are documented and used in daily work.

### Level 3: Permission Clarity

Permissions for access, contribution, execution, review, approval, objection, escalation, and control are defined.

### Level 4: Decision Integration

Roles and permissions are connected to explicit decision rights, RACI mappings, approval thresholds, and escalation paths.

### Level 5: Evidence-Based Governance

Role execution, decisions, reviews, controls, and escalations are documented through structured, reviewable, and auditable evidence.

## Documentation Expectations

The Role, Function and Permission Model should be documented in a structured and versionable way.

Typical artifacts include:

* role catalog
* function map
* permission matrix
* interface map
* separation of duties matrix
* RACI mappings
* decision rights mappings
* escalation paths
* control responsibility map
* stakeholder participation map

All governance-relevant changes should be traceable, reviewable, and versioned.

---

## Review and Evolution

Roles, functions, permissions, and interfaces should be reviewed regularly.

Review triggers may include:

* new Epics
* changed strategic priorities
* changed stakeholder groups
* new regulatory or compliance expectations
* major architecture changes
* operational incidents
* repeated escalation patterns
* capacity constraints
* audit findings
* delivery delays
* service level violations

The model should evolve iteratively as organizational complexity, risk, stakeholder impact, and operational maturity increase.

---

## Summary

The Role, Function and Permission Model provides the organizational foundation for accountable, transparent, and cross-functional execution within the Agile Innovation Framework.

It defines who contributes, which functions are required, which permissions apply, where responsibilities begin and end, how expertise is integrated, and how governance interfaces connect strategy, product, architecture, implementation, operations, compliance, security, stakeholders, control, escalation, and arbitration.

It enables organizations to keep agile innovation adaptive, while making responsibility, authority boundaries, participation, and control explicit.
